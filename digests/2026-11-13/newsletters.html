AI as Normal Technology

The missing quadrant in responses to AI — Nov 10, 2026

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor announce STABL (Societal Transformation and AI Bottlenecks Lab), a new nonprofit research organization. They lay out a 2x2 in which "technological determinists" (and some AI critics) expect superintelligence to sweep away every downstream bottleneck, while defenders of the status quo treat institutions as fixed. STABL claims the neglected top-right quadrant: it treats AI as transformative and asks how institutions can be reformed so its benefits arrive. Its agenda includes measuring progress toward recursive self-improvement with open-ended evaluations (CRUX; frontier agents' research papers have so far been rejected by expert reviewers), studying diffusion and labor impacts, and making resilience, not just alignment, central to AI risk policy.

Hyperdimensional (Dean Ball)

Iron Makes Iron — Nov 10, 2026

Ball, now writing in a personal capacity after joining OpenAI, responds to METR's finding that Anthropic is improving its models on well-specified tasks about twice as fast as in 2024–25, and to Anthropic's S-1 warning that it may soon hit its RSP threshold for "dramatically accelerated progress." He distinguishes "weak recursion," where a technology is a key input to its own improvement (iron making iron, AI helping build AI, which is historically normal), from "strong recursion," where a technology autonomously plans and builds its successor. He sees good evidence for the first and almost none for the second, since humans still specify the tasks. He supports rules that keep humans in practical control of AI development, but warns that poorly calibrated panic could produce nuclear-style centralization and lost benefits.

One Useful Thing (Ethan Mollick)

Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI — Nov 9, 2026

After pre-launch access to Claude Fable 5.2, Mollick asks whether it counts as AGI ("as good as most human experts at most intellectual tasks you can do with a computer"), and answers "almost": Jagged AGI. His tests: a one-shot code-made video, hacking a USB keypad into a six-game console, a business plan (Prebid Brief) built from 201 local governments' agendas that he says would impress an MBA team, and a reconstruction of Auden's notorious 1941 literature course. The failures are no longer hallucinations but judgment and taste: a joyless course, a 182-page paper, self-imposed bureaucracy. Most of all, the model never noticed these problems on its own, even with critic and fact-checker agents on nearly every project.

The Argument

Subpoena Anthropic — Nov 8, 2026

In this week's Closing Argument, Kelsey Piper says METR's report on Anthropic is the biggest recent update on AI risk, but most of its evidence was redacted at Anthropic's request as "commercially sensitive." What remains says Anthropic expects RSI very soon, that a year of progress on well-specified tasks now takes about six months, and that staff report working roughly 12x faster with internal models. That makes "AI 2027" look too cautious. Piper argues Congress should obtain the unredacted report through its procedures for nonpublic information, have RSI believers and skeptics vet it, and ban RSI if the claims hold up.

Mark Zuckerberg's race to the bottom — Nov 9, 2026

Kobe Yank-Jacobs argues that Meta, now a frontier competitor with Muse Wonder and Muse personal agents, threatens to turn Anthropic's "race to the top" into a race to the bottom. He reads Zuckerberg's "The Future Is for Everyone" essay and its "personal superintelligence" and "checks and balances" framing as either cover for Meta's business interests (distillation protections, compute advantage) or a confused view that misses the core alignment problem. He also cites reporting that Meta rushed Muse's launch with "half-baked protections" once a rival shipped, as evidence that "natural incentives" won't keep labs safe without regulation.

WIRED AI Lab (Will Knight)

Dummies like me can build robot ducks now — Nov 11, 2026

Knight used GPT-6 Sol, Nous Research's Hermes agent harness and Nvidia's Isaac Sim to train a simulated Microduck robot to walk and spot his Bengal cat in a few hours. The model wrote the code, watched videos of the robot and tuned its training. He reports that Berkeley's Ken Goldberg and Nvidia's Jim Fan are seeing similar gains: GPT-6 Astra built full 3D robot simulations from a video clip in an hour, and their newly open-sourced Aspire framework let it learn skills such as solving a Rubik's cube with two arms. Knight predicts a "vibe robotics" wave. Also covered: the SRE-Bench reverse-engineering benchmark, and a study finding AI peer reviewers miss errors of omission.
