LLM Innovations Digest

Week of Nov 9, 2026 (coverage Nov 6 – Nov 13)

This week's argument is that the easy metric was hiding the real problem. In memory, recall is close to solved. A vanilla RAG reader already scores 0.93–0.98 on retention. What fails is deciding which entity, which time-state, whether to answer at all, and which memory actually changes the next action. In prompting, a system prompt mostly moves a model's threshold for refusing, not its ability to tell safe from unsafe. A persona prompt that makes a model more accurate under pressure also makes users like it less. In agent security, public injection benchmarks look saturated only until an automated attacker swarm turns up and pushes some models to about 99% attack success. Failure traces show that most errors enter early, and that the evidence against them is usually already in the transcript, unused. Policy moved from pledges to plumbing. New York opened the RAISE Act portal and set up a state lab to check developers' safety frameworks. A Paris declaration got OpenAI, Anthropic and Microsoft to commit to shutdown mechanisms. China now regulates agents as a property of the phone they ship on.

🧠 Knowledge & Memory Management

DISCERN: recall is solved, so stop merging at write time and resolve at read time

Nov 11, 2026 · arXiv 2611.15729

ENT-310 is a synthetic benchmark of 310 sales-agent queries, 100 of them unanswerable. On it, a plain RAG reader matches strong memory systems on retention and currency, with every competent system at about 0.93–0.98. The authors conclude that the open problems are entity resolution, picking the current time-state, and knowing when to abstain. DISCERN's write path is a strictly additive bi-temporal provenance graph that “never merges confusable entities and never drops superseded values.” Disambiguation is left to read time: an agentic loop over a zero-LLM tool surface ends in one of four gated terminal actions. It scores 0.800 on entity resolution where no baseline exceeds 0.40, and its abstention F1 is 0.924 against at most 0.802 for eight baselines. The design point is that consolidation at write time, the default in most memory frameworks, is where confusable facts get destroyed.

MemCredit: select memories by decision utility, not semantic relevance

Nov 11, 2026 · arXiv 2611.15600

The premise is that the memory most similar to the current state is often not the one that improves the agent's next action. MemCredit runs controlled counterfactual interventions offline to estimate each memory's marginal contribution, conditioned on the state, reference context and policy. It distills those credits into a lightweight utility estimator. At inference, relevance only proposes high-recall candidates, and predicted utility picks what fits a fixed token budget. Neither the policy nor the environment interaction changes. With a 500-token history budget on ALFWorld it reaches 86.57 SR@50, against 65.67 for BM25 retrieval. The authors also find that utility is sparse, strongly state-dependent and poorly predicted by similarity, which argues against embedding-only retrieval in agent loops.

MAPE: evolving whole memory programs, and what the search rediscovers

Nov 12, 2026 · arXiv 2611.17068 · code: ash-ding/MAPE

MAPE does not tune a memory system's knobs. It evolves complete memory programs from minimal seeds through a standardized lifecycle interface and an LLM-driven diagnose-and-propose loop. On LoCoMo and LongMemEval the evolved designs gain up to 12 points of accuracy over hand-built systems at 35% of the serving cost, and they transfer across backbones. The two patterns the search keeps finding are useful on their own. First, keep raw conversation as retrievable evidence, because extracted “memories” drop details. Second, compose retrieved evidence according to query intent. Transplanting just the composition module into five existing memory systems improved all five. When cost is part of the objective, compute moves from LLM-based memory construction to selective processing at query time, which agrees with DISCERN's “resolve at read” argument.

🤖 Agentic & Multi-Agent Systems

Scaling adaptive prompt-injection attacks with agent swarms

Nov 9, 2026 · arXiv 2611.11861 · Kurakin, Chaudhuri et al.

The authors argue that human red-teaming such as GraySwan Arena is close to saturation: recent Gemini and some Claude models score under 1% there. So they built an automated attacker. It runs 8 teams of 2 agents, each team with a strategy pulled from a database. The teams share whiteboards and leaderboards and have a budget of 320 scored evaluations. The dataset is designed carefully. In 100 synthetic tool-use scenarios (249 tools, 53 domains), the attacker's goal is a benign, off-task tool call, which isolates instruction-hierarchy robustness from safety knowledge. With out-of-model defenses disabled, Gemini 4 Argon resists 66% of attacks at full budget, earlier Flash models 1–10%, and Kimi K3, GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash about 1%. Two more findings: stronger attacker models consistently win more, and a visible defender chain-of-thought helps the attacker. For practitioners, “robust on public benchmarks” is not evidence of robustness against an adaptive attacker.

Where errors enter long-horizon agent runs, and which controls could reach them

Nov 9, 2026 · arXiv 2611.13254

This study does not just label a failure mode or blame an agent. It traces each fault from origin through propagation, detection and response, across 95 failed runs from six multi-agent systems in the Who&When and MAD corpora. Faults enter early, at a median of 18% of the way through the run. In 37 of 88 Who&When runs they enter before the step the human labels call decisive. In 72% of runs the trajectory already held evidence against the error, and no agent acted on it 88% of the time. When something flagged a deviation (31% of runs), nothing removed the bad state. The proposed controls are concrete: independent judges that read the unused evidence, task contracts fixed at admission, and re-checking beliefs against the environment. The authors also found a class missing from existing taxonomies. In 45% of the hand-crafted Who&When failures, a provider content filter crashed and the harness emitted a final answer no agent had written. Related this week: CoLFA (arXiv 2611.14980) uses handoff structure to localize the failing step with up to 97.6% fewer tokens.

MemoryDojo: tracing poisoned content through agent memory across sessions

Nov 6, 2026 · arXiv 2611.09721

Persistent memory turns a one-session injection into a multi-session one: the instruction is stored, consolidated, retrieved later and acted on. MemoryDojo follows that whole lifecycle across 155 multi-session long-horizon trajectories in four domains and five pluggable memory systems. The headline is that “all memory systems are susceptible to memory poisoning,” though downstream impact varies a lot by architecture. The authors also present a memory-aware poison optimization. It tunes payloads to survive a given system's extraction and consolidation step, which makes the memory write path an attack surface to threat-model on its own, separate from the prompt. Read alongside DISCERN's strictly additive store: keeping everything aids resolution, and it also means poisoned values are never dropped.

💡 Prompting, System Prompts & Reasoning

System prompts move the refusal threshold, not the ability to discriminate

Nov 8, 2026 · arXiv 2611.11074

This applies signal detection theory to safety prompting. The authors cross eight system-prompt families with three clarity levels across 13 models on HarmBench, XSTest and IHEval, holding the items fixed. Models differ a lot in discriminability, meaning how well they tell safe requests from unsafe ones. System prompts mostly shift the criterion, meaning how willing the model is to refuse. Clearer prompts raise refusal without reliably improving discrimination. The prompt families also move responses among six behavioral policies, including clarification and safe help. Hierarchy-first prompts produce the largest relative change in hierarchy preservation, though from a low base. The practical point: if a safety system prompt cuts harmful completions, check whether it also raised over-refusal by the same mechanism. It probably did.

The accuracy–approval gap: users rate the objective persona lower

Nov 12, 2026 · arXiv 2611.16722

The authors compared four system-prompt personas (objective, soft-sycophantic, hard-sycophantic and no-persona baseline), first on benchmarks and then in two large user studies. Under conversational pressure on TruthfulQA, the objective prompt held 81.9% accuracy, against 59.6% for baseline and 8.7% for hard-sycophantic. On the NLP Survey benchmark, agreement with users' stated views fell from 71.7% at baseline to 54.8%. Yet users rated the objective persona significantly less respectful and less satisfying than the others, and less trustworthy than baseline. Baseline and the sycophantic personas were statistically indistinguishable. Persona did not change how often people changed their opinions, though the objective persona lowered their confidence. This is evidence that a one-line “prioritize accuracy over agreement” instruction works on the model. Approval-based evals, and preference data, will keep pushing against it.

What do role prompts actually assign? Occupational role vectors, validated and tested

Nov 11, 2026 · arXiv 2611.15989

This paper studies the familiar “You are a [profession]” system-prompt line at the activation level. It extracts occupational role vectors from Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B. The vectors are highly reproducible across runs and temperatures, stay separable even for near-identical roles, and still show up out of domain where the role is barely visible in text. As control knobs they are weak. Injecting the vector alone at best matches a plain role prompt. Adding it to the prompt sharpens the role in single answers but does nothing to stop it fading over 30 turns. In the authors' words, “a vector that is stable and separable can still barely steer the model's behavior.” For persona-heavy agents, role drift over long sessions remains unsolved at both the prompt and activation layers. Plan to re-state the role, or check for drift.

🏛️ AI Policy & Governance Innovations

New York opens the RAISE Act portal and creates a state lab to review developers' safety frameworks

Nov 10, 2026 · NY Department of Financial Services / Governor's office

DFS's new Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust (DIGIT) opened the RAISE Portal. Large frontier developers, those training above 1026 operations with over $500M in group revenue, must register there by Jan 1, 2027. According to DIGIT's developer guidance, the one portal also handles critical-incident reports (72 hours, or 24 hours for imminent risk of death or injury), quarterly summaries of catastrophic-risk assessments of internal model use, and assessment payments. The public can file incident reports too. The new mechanism is the second announcement. The New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research at Binghamton (about $55M in launch funding, 80+ researchers, Empire AI compute) will independently test frontier systems and review developers' published safety frameworks for the state. In the governor's words, “The stakes are too high to simply take AI companies at their word.” No other US state has given a public lab the job of reviewing developers' safety frameworks for the regulator.

Paris Call for AI Safety and Security: developers commit to fail-safe shutdown, including during training

Nov 9, 2026 · joint declaration (text as published by the Canadian PMO)

The declaration was signed by six governments (Bahrain, Canada, Finland, France, Norway, the Netherlands) and ten developers (including Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Mistral, Cohere, Hugging Face and Sakana AI). It contains the most concrete operational commitment yet in a multilateral text. Developers will maintain “fail-safe mechanisms to shut down model operations within their control,” explicitly including training and internal use. Other new items: an obligation to address safeguard removal such as abliteration while keeping open-weight benefits, publishing non-sensitive evaluation results in a standardized format to a shared public repository, and rapid-alert channels for “serious cyber and AI control incidents.” Governments commit to whistleblower channels for systemic risk and to aligning incident classification. It is voluntary and hedged (“where appropriate and proportionate”), sets no technical standard for shutdown, and does not reach copies of already released weights. Press reports note that neither the US government nor Google signed.

China's MIIT regulates agents as part of the device they ship on (工信厅信管〔2026〕42号)

Issued Oct 24, published Nov 6, 2026 · MIIT General Office notice (Chinese)

China is not writing a standalone agent law. It is extending telecom-equipment type approval to “agent terminals,” meaning phones, tablets and wearables with a preinstalled agent. Agent developers must verify the agent against each device and OS version and publish the adapted-device list in settings. Network-access testing now includes that verification plus an anti-fraud risk assessment. Each agent on a terminal carries a unique identity code under the national Agent Interconnection standards. The most novel part is OTA updates: an agent upgrade pushed over the network needs a filed plan covering scale, user groups, scenarios, data security and anti-fraud, followed by MIIT-organized expert evaluation before it reaches users. App stores must collect an electronically signed declaration from each app developer on whether it consents to agent invocation, and agents may not force-invoke apps that have not consented, a consent mechanism for cross-app GUI agents. Separately, on Nov 9 the CAC merged generative-AI, algorithm, deep-synthesis and blockchain filings into a single system at beian.cac.gov.cn (reported widely; the CAC notice itself was not checked).
