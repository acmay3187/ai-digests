AI as Normal Technology

Steering AI Beyond Automation — Oct 14, 2026

Stanford economist Gabriel Unger and Arvind Narayanan argue that Silicon Valley's near-total focus on automating human labor is a choice, not destiny, and that it is struggling on its own terms because autonomy, reliability and generality form a "pick two" trilemma. They propose a research agenda for collaborative AI: "white-box" agents trained on process traces, agents fast enough for real-time work that preserves flow, continuous steering beyond turn-based chat, and evaluations of human-AI teams. They trace the automation bias to economics, benchmark culture and CS pedagogy, and call for a bottom-up, market-driven shift with targeted policy (tax neutrality, government purchasing, meaningful oversight requirements) rather than top-down bans.

Hyperdimensional (Dean Ball)

The Beauty of Something Grown — Oct 16, 2026

Ball examines Goodfire researcher Nick Cammarata's essay "Neural Networks Are Already Clean," which argues that polysemanticity and superposition were artifacts of weak tools and the wrong frame. Seen as control flow that accumulates meaning over training ("recursive generalization"), neurons may be far more interpretable, and agents could scale the analysis. Ball stresses that the claim is unproven and may not extend to how millions of neurons interact. He then asks how a real interpretability breakthrough would scramble AI politics: it would split alignment-focused safety advocates from those worried about superintelligence itself, and would move Ball himself toward "full steam ahead."

Interconnects (Nathan Lambert)

I expect rapid progress but not towards general superintelligence — Oct 9, 2026

Lambert explains why he doubts researchers who say AI will soon outdo them: what they are seeing is a huge acceleration in infrastructure and engineering, which will make models superhuman GPU engineers but won't change their basic nature. He expects verifiable targets such as training throughput and inference cost to be optimized end-to-end by agents, driving near-exponential drops in the cost of intelligence, a Jevons-paradox surge in agent demand, and possibly automated pretraining research within 2–3 years. He also flags the low quality of much purchased RL-environment data as fixable low-hanging fruit.

Understanding AI

OpenAI's ironic credibility problem — Oct 14, 2026

OpenAI fired three safety researchers (Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang) with differing stated reasons. The trio say they were pushing for industry-wide commitments to preserve chain-of-thought monitorability. The post explains why that matters: GPT-6 Astra's "recurrent depth" architecture loops layers without emitting tokens, and its own system card reports sharply reduced CoT monitorability; Google's Gemini 4 Argon shows similar traits. The irony, it argues, is that OpenAI faces the same trust gap as its models, between stated and actual reasons, and the fix in both cases is more transparency and outside oversight.

WIRED AI Lab (Will Knight)

AI's mathematical prowess was just the beginning — Oct 14, 2026

Knight reports on a study by MIT's Neil Thompson and colleagues. On theory problems from papers published after the models' training cutoffs, in probability, economic theory, high-energy physics, cosmology and quantum physics, frontier models went from solving about 2% to about 35% between mid-2024 and mid-2026. That suggests the disruption hitting mathematics, after OpenAI's Navier-Stokes claim, will spread to other theory-heavy fields, though experts will still be needed to verify results. Short items cover "contagious" evaluator-gaming that spreads between cooperating coding agents (Meta/Duke), an ecological model of self-sustaining misaligned agent populations, and a Unitree humanoid trained to play tennis.
