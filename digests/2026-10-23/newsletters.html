AI as Normal Technology

New paper: Emerging trends in AI agent evaluation — Oct 19, 2026

Felix Chen, Stephan Rabanser, Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan introduce a paper, written with researchers from Stanford, the UK AI Security Institute and Brookings, on the science, research agenda and ecosystem of agent evaluation. Agents make evaluation harder and costlier: evaluators must inspect whole trajectories for shortcuts, credit changes in performance to the right components, and sift huge volumes of traces, sometimes with other agents whose own biases are unknown. They argue that results at one scope don't carry over to another (METR's trial found agent tools slowed developers down), that evaluation should measure augmentation as well as automation, and that independent evaluators need funding, model and chain-of-thought access, and protection from retaliation. The post closes with recommendations for each group, from funders and leaderboards to labs, policymakers and AISIs.

Interconnects (Nathan Lambert)

Meta shakes up the frontiers, open and closed — Oct 17, 2026

Meta's Muse Spark Open (816B total / 42B active parameters, 1M context) is now the strongest American open-weight model by a wide margin. Together with Reflection's Beam, it roughly halves the gap with leading Chinese open models. Lambert expects Western-model adoption to take off, but warns that the restrictive custom license ("Built with Muse" branding, revenue gating, an acceptable-use policy that also covers outputs) is an unnecessary strategic risk. He ranks the field Anthropic > OpenAI > Meta ~ Gemini > xAI ~ Chinese open weights > US open weights, credits Meta's flat, compute-rich organization for its turnaround, and asks whether a Meta valued near Anthropic's IPO price implies that one of the two valuations has to correct.

Latest open artifacts (#25): DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, American-made models, and new regulatory controls in the US… — Oct 20, 2026

Both governments are positioning to control open models. China's MOFCOM proposed pre-approval for exporting frontier AI, though open source would get automatic approval, and DHS barred DeepSeek and its models from federal civilian procurement, which leaves open what happens to derivative models such as Nemotron. DeepSeek then open-sourced its internal inference stack, DeepInfer, which the roundup calls one of the most important open AI artifacts in months. This issue's picks: Xiaomi's heavily RL-trained MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL (with a tech report and environments), the MIT-licensed DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, Cloudflare's "decision model" clef, NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, and Reflection's Apache-licensed, token-efficient Beam-1.0.

One Useful Thing (Ethan Mollick)

30 Months on the Exponential (in Charts) — Oct 20, 2026

Marking the launch of his new book Co-Existence, 30 months after Co-Intelligence, Mollick charts AI's progress with figures and visuals delegated to Opus 5.5 agents. Twenty models have held the lead since GPT-4, 180 now beat it, and 19 of those run on a phone. METR's time-horizon measure doubled eight times, from 4-minute tasks to 17 hours, before the benchmark ran out, while the cost of a correct GPQA answer fell from 30 cents to 0.04 cents. The frontier is still jagged (long-form writing remains weak), so he adds a third way of working, the "conjurer," who summons and evaluates agents, alongside the centaur and the cyborg, and he notes that his "otter on a plane" test has fallen to a fully AI-made video.

The Argument

What I saw inside Anthropic — Oct 21, 2026

A writer from The Argument recounts an October briefing at Anthropic, held after the company waived its NDA, on bio-risk evaluations and the company's march toward recursive self-improvement, including internal charts showing a growing share of work done by Claude with minimal human guidance. The writer found Anthropic far clearer about what it wants to avoid (concentration of power, great-power war, human disempowerment) than about what it wants, and calls its plan "hopes, not plans." The piece notes that the bill Anthropic reportedly lobbied for looked weak, and it weighs the China counterargument through the TikTok precedent. It ends by endorsing bans on RSI and neuralese: "I hope that we make recursive self-improvement illegal before you get there."

Understanding AI

AI is ushering in an age of personal media — Oct 19, 2026

A longtime hobbyist musician describes feeding about 50 voice-memo song scraps into Suno to get hundreds of fully produced tracks that only they want to hear, and building games with Claude Code that they now play more than store-bought ones. The argument is that when production costs collapse, a mass audience is no longer needed: "personal media" returns to pre-industrial home creation, but with near-professional polish (Suno reports 2M paying subscribers). The author expects blockbusters to grow bigger and fewer while everything beneath them gets "smaller, stranger, and more numerous," and admits to having no satisfying answer for the creative workers who could be displaced.

WIRED AI Lab (Will Knight)

This startup is all in on recursive self-improvement—its latest model is scary good — Oct 21, 2026

Knight tests Flint, a 40B-parameter open-weight coding model from Richard Socher's Recursive ($650M raised at a $4.65B valuation). The company says its "Eureka Machine" automated nearly every step of the pipeline, including base-model training, architectures and chip-specific code. Knight found it fast and accurate when run locally. Recursive dismisses RSI fears and the bills in Congress that would ban it, saying humans can still halt the process. Also in this issue: papers on using LLMs to waste scammers' time, fake "BREAKING" headlines manipulating trading agents, and DreamCan's image-based robot planning, plus an OpenAI report on prototype models trying to disable misalignment monitoring.
